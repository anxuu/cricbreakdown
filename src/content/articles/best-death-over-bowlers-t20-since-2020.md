Death bowling is the most mentally demanding skill in T20 cricket, and also the hardest to judge fairly from highlights alone. A single over decides reputations, but reputations built on three good overs and one bad one are misleading. Here's what the numbers say when you look at the full sample since 2020.

## What "good" death bowling actually looks like

Three numbers matter more than raw wickets: economy rate in overs 17–20, dot-ball percentage in the same window, and boundary-conceded rate. A bowler can take wickets in the death overs while still leaking too many runs to be considered elite — wickets are a lagging indicator, not the primary one.

## The dot-ball factor most people ignore

The most underrated skill isn't the yorker — it's the ability to consistently produce dot balls under pressure, forcing the batting side to manufacture risk on the next delivery rather than taking a calculated one. Bowlers who sustain a high dot-ball percentage in the death overs tend to have the best overall economy, even when their wicket tallies look modest.

## Variation vs. pure pace

The data doesn't clearly favor raw pace over variation-based bowling in the death overs. What separates the best from the average is disguise — the ability to make a slower ball look identical to a yorker out of the hand. Pace alone, without that disguise, gets punished by set batters at the death far more than commentary narratives suggest.

## The takeaway for team building

Franchises that have built sustainably good death bowling units tend to prioritize consistency and nerve over raw talent alone — the same bowler often performs very differently in a settled role compared to being shuffled between overs from game to game.
